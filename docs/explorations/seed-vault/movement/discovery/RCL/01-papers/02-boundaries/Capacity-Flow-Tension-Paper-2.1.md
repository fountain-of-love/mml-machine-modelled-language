# The Capacity–Flow Tension

## Why Physical Systems Must Both Contain and Release Energy

### Paper 2.1 — Developed Draft

> This document is a developed working draft. Its equations, scope, provenance, maturity assignments, and novelty boundary remain subject to review.

## Central Question

> What boundary conditions allow a substrate to hold energy without preventing its transformation?

## Core Proposition

A physical system becomes capable through containment but becomes effective through flow.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Containment}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{Flow}
}
$$

The tension is an **inner–outer relation**. The inner system provides inherent storage and organization; the relevant outer system provides source, load, field, reservoir, or surrounding degrees of freedom through which that capacity is activated. Both sides belong to the energy ledger. The boundary does not separate an energetic system from an irrelevant exterior; it identifies where energy is stored, transferred, coupled, dissipated, and observed.

$$
\boxed{
\text{inner capacity}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{boundary coupling}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{outer capacity}
}
$$

## Two Nested Two-Store Structures

The phrase “two-store system” has two related but non-identical meanings.

### Modal dual storage

Inside an admitted linear resonant mode, two complementary recoverable stores exchange energy:

$$
H_{\mathrm{mode}}
=
H_{\mathcal I}+H_{\mathcal C}
=
\frac12\mathcal I f^2
+
\frac{q^2}{2\mathcal C}.
$$

These are the inertive and compliant aspects of one selected mode. In mechanics they appear as kinetic and elastic storage; in electromagnetism as magnetic and electric storage; in acoustics and hydraulics as bulk motion and compression; in second sound as counterflow motion and thermal displacement.

### Systemic inner–outer storage

At the larger open-system level, the selected system interacts with a relevant surrounding system:

$$
\boxed{
H_{\mathrm{extended}}
=
H_{\mathrm{inner}}
+
H_{\mathrm{coupling}}
+
H_{\mathrm{outer}}
}
$$

where the partition is defined by the chosen boundary. Its balance is

$$
\boxed{
\dot H_{\mathrm{extended}}
=
P_{\mathrm{external source}}
-P_{\mathrm{external sink}}
-D_{\mathrm{extended}}.
}
$$

For the inner subsystem alone,

$$
\boxed{
\dot H_{\mathrm{inner}}
=
P_{\mathrm{outer}\rightarrow\mathrm{inner}}
-P_{\mathrm{inner}\rightarrow\mathrm{outer}}
-D_{\mathrm{inner}}.
}
$$

The coupling energy must be retained when the boundary interaction itself has memory or storage. It may be negligible in an ideal port, localized in an interface, or distributed across a field. It cannot be counted simultaneously as inner and outer energy.

The modal and systemic decompositions are nested:

$$
\boxed{
H_{\mathcal I}+H_{\mathcal C}
\subseteq
H_{\mathrm{inner}}+H_{\mathrm{coupling}}+H_{\mathrm{outer}}.
}
$$

Thus the two canonical modal stores should not automatically be renamed “inner” and “outer.” Often both lie inside the selected boundary. The broader truth is that resonantly realized capacity requires an internally admissible mode **and** an energetic relation with an outer system that activates, loads, or observes it.

## Intrinsic, Relational, and Realized Capacity

The nested view distinguishes three capacity states:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal K_{\mathrm{intrinsic}}
&=\kappa\Sigma(\Psi\cdot1)^2,\\
\mathcal K_{\mathrm{relational}}
&=\kappa\Sigma(\Psi G_r)^2,\\
\mathcal K_{\mathrm{realized}}
&=\mathcal K_{\mathrm{relational}}
\quad\text{subject to source, boundary, loss, and regime constraints.}
\end{aligned}
}
$$

Intrinsic capacity names what the inner substrate carries under the neutral transformation. Relational capacity names the organized storage reached through a defined system–environment interaction. Realized capacity names what is physically attained after the full energy balance and limits are applied.

This is not permission to add intrinsic and relational expressions indiscriminately. If the relational expression already denotes the total state reached by activating the same store, then adding the intrinsic value again would double-count it. An additive decomposition is valid only when the underlying Hamiltonian supplies distinct, non-overlapping terms.

## Resonant Capacity as the Boundary-Activated Member

For the admitted quadratic two-store class,

$$
\mathcal I\dot f+\mathcal Rf+\frac q{\mathcal C}=e_s,
\qquad
\dot q=f,
$$

the inner mode supplies $\Sigma=\mathcal I$ and the quadratic storage metric. The outer source, load, and boundary determine the activation protocol and enter the derived transfer gain

$$
G_r=\frac{f_r}{\Psi}.
$$

The resulting role-preserving capacity law is

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal K_r
=
\kappa\Sigma(\Psi G_r)^2.
}
$$

This gives the formula its systemic reading:

> Capacity is the inner substrate's ability to receive and hold an organized effect, realized through an energetically explicit relation with an outer system; resonance quantifies the coherent amplification produced by that declared interaction.

The source does not create capacity from nothing. It transfers energy into an admissible inner storage structure. The inner structure does not realize resonant capacity in isolation. Both the receiving mode and its outer energetic relation are required.

## Physical Expression

Stored energy:

$$
\boxed{E=\mathcal H(x)}
$$

Driving difference:

$$
\boxed{e=-\nabla\Phi}
$$

Power transfer:

$$
\boxed{P=ef}
$$

Energy balance:

$$
\boxed{
\dot E
=
P_{\mathrm{in}}-P_{\mathrm{out}}
}
$$

Without containment:

$$
E_{\mathrm{stored}}\rightarrow0.
$$

Without flow:

$$
P=ef\rightarrow0.
$$

Realized capacity therefore requires both:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Realized capacity}
=
\text{Ability to hold}
\times
\text{Ability to transfer}
}
$$

This expression is a semantic seed rather than a universal dimensional equation; each domain binding must define how holding and transfer combine physically.

## Drift at Either Pole

Excess containment may produce rigidity, isolation, accumulation without expression, suppressed transfer, or equilibrium that cannot adapt.

Excess flow may produce leakage, instability, loss of state, inability to store, or uncontrolled dispersion.

## The Capacity–Flow Dance

**How containment makes energy possible and permeability makes it active.**

> A physical system becomes capable through containment, but becomes effective through flow.

## Cross-Cutting Regime Condition

Near equilibrium, boundaries and storage coefficients may be treated as stable and flows may remain proportional to gradients. Far from equilibrium, flow can alter the substrate, boundary, or constitutive relation itself.

## Evidence Intake 001 — Nominal versus Realized Capacity

This paper must distinguish nominal or intrinsic capacity, state-dependent stored energy or exergy, source-deliverable activation over a stated interval, and realized response allowed by material, boundary, dissipation, and nonlinear constraints.

“Ability to hold $\times$ ability to transfer” remains semantic. A testable realized-capacity statement must be derived from a state equation and boundary balance. For a selected recoverable store one may write

$$
\dot E_{\mathrm{store}}
=P_{\mathrm{in}}-P_{\mathrm{out}}-P_{\mathrm{conversion}},
$$

where converted energy remains in the broader energy ledger unless it crosses the system boundary. The proposed minimum-of-limits expression remains a design intuition until all limits are defined on compatible quantities, feasible states, and time horizons.

## Evidence Intake 002 — Source and Limit Conditions

The canonical resonator shows why a source limit cannot be appended after the amplitude calculation as an unrelated cap. With fixed source effort, decreasing resistance increases both response and input power. With fixed source power, stored energy instead scales as $QP_{\mathrm{in}}/\omega_0$.

Material, source, nonlinear, and boundary “capacities” are not generally scalar stores of the same kind. A rigorous bounded problem can instead be posed as

$$
H_{\mathrm{realizable}}
=
\sup_{x,u}
\left\{
H(x):
\text{dynamics, source, boundary, material, and stability constraints hold}
\right\}.
$$

The simpler minimum notation may remain explanatory shorthand only after each limit has been mapped into a compatible upper bound on the same storage quantity $H$.
